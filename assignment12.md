## Lede

The story opens with a timeline account of the Camp Fire in Paradise, CA. It starts on the power line snapping in the harsh wind, then follows the large fire as it moves into town. The first couple paragraphs affirm the sheer scale of this disaster, including the 85 deaths and almost 19,000 buildings that were destroyed.

I think this lede is effective because it gives the readers a crystal clear picture of what happened before bringing up the larger issue. The line that mentions people claiming that nobody could have predicted the disaster also successfully sets up the main argument. The argument is that the risks were not actually unpredictable. The story then moves from Paradise to the much larger question of how many other Western communities could face similar dangers.

I think the lede works because it makes the data feel more relevant. Rather than starting with a basic statistic about wildfire risk across a broad 11 states, it begins with one specific disaster and then shows why the same thing could happen somewhere else. It did take a few paragraphs to get to the main discoveries, but I think the opening is engaging enough to justify that length.

## The data

The story uses data from the U.S. Forest Service to compare wildfire hazards across 11 Western states. The analysis examines around 5,000 communities and uses 65 different risk factors, including topography, rainfall, vegetation and past fires. The data also includes simulations of tens of thousands of possible fire seasons varying by severity.

The reporters then took this information and combined it with census data to assess the risks that face individual communities. They created a one-mile buffer zone around each community to take into consideration the distance that wind-blown embers can move. Each community received a hazard score based on the average rating of the burnable land within the respective area.

The story uses the Paradise fire as a point of compariosn. Its wildfire hazard score was 3.81 out of of 1 to 5. The analysis found that 526 communities across the West had a higher score, most of which were smaller than Paradise.

However, the reporters make it very clear that wildfire hazard by itself does not tell everything. They also looked into any factors that could make a fire more dangerous for the residents, including evacuation routes, age, disabilities, emergency alert systems and trailer parks. I think this is important because it shows that the main danger is not just how likely a fire is to happen. It, too, is also about whether or not people can get out safely when a fire does happen.

## Sources

The story used a mix of government data, academic expertise, fire officials and people who experienced the Camp Fire firsthand. The U.S. Forest Service data provided the foundation for this analysis, while the researchers and other officials helped explain what the findings mean when applied.

One of these examples is Phillip Levin, a University of Washington researcher/lead scientist for the Nature Conservancy in Washington. He discussed the difference between a natural fire and a human disaster. 

"Fire is natural. But the disaster happens because people didn't know to leave, or couldn't leave. It didn't have to happen” was a quote I liked.

I thought this was a good quote because it helped explain the argument the story was making. The reporters are both simply measuring where fires are most likely to occur and examining how communities are prepared to deal with them.

The story also uses people that were directly involved with the evacuation in Paradise. Phil John, who is a Fire Safe Council leader who helped develop the evacuation plans before the Camp Fire, explained that residents had already been warned about the danger but the plans eventually collapsed when the fire began to spread.

These sources help connect the data to real experiences. It also gave the story a human element that could have been missing from an analysis based on lots of communities and different risk factors.

## Story structure

I think the story uses a mix of chronological storytelling and a martini glass structure. It starts off by describing the Camp Fire and the destruction seen in Paradise. After laying out the initial disaster, it zooms out to explain the data analysis and the number of communities facing similar fire risks.

From there, the story breaks the issue into different sections, including evacuation routes, problems with older residents, disabilities, alert systems and the aforementioned mobile home parks. It then goes back to the issue of Paradise to explain these risks further.

Using a series of specific community-based examples to describe how the larger findings are applied in different places. This prevents the analysis from looking like a list of basic stats about the dangers of wildfires. I think the structure works overall because it moves between what was found and specific examples without getting lost trying to describe  the main point.

## Numbers

I thought the reporters did a good job of making the numbers understandable for a story about data. There are a lot of statistics throughout the story, but most of them are connected to an explanation of why they are important.

The story also gives the numbers contextual meanings by comparing them to the actual numeric outcome of the Camp Fire. It says that at least 62 of the 85 people who died were older than 65, and that 36 were over the age of 75. It also says that 37 of the people killed lived in mobile homes, placing an emphasis on the risk that comes with mobile home parks.

I liked that the story did not only provide these numbers without explaining why they mattered. The statistics helped show how age, disability and housing conditions can affect a person's ability to survive a wildfire.

There are some sections where the amount of data becomes a little overwhelming, especially when the story moves through the different risk factors. However, the headings and visualizations help break up the information and make it easier to follow.

## Data visualizations

The story uses plenty of data visualizations, which I thought made sense given how much information the reporters collected. The maps in specific are useful because they allowed the readers to see which communities had higher wildfire hazard scores located across the American West.

I also liked the interactive feature that allows readers to search for individual communities. Since the analysis covers about 5,000 places across 11 states, it would be difficult to communicate every finding through text alone. I have family in some of the states mentioned and was morbidly curious to see the risk in their communities. 

The visualizations also help explain some of the more specific findings. For example, the map showing communities with higher wildfire hazard scores than Paradise gives readers a sense of how widespread the danger is. Other graphics show evacuation routes, the locations of mobile home parks and the ages of people who died in the Camp Fire.

I do think that the consistency of the visualizations could have been improved. Some of the graphics are easily understandable compared to others, and some readers might need to spend time figuring out exactly what is being compared and contrasted. I also think the story could have done more to explain the relation between different risk factors. A community might have a high wildfire hazard score but good evacuation options, while another might have fewer viable options for residents to escape.

## Methods

The story explained its methodology pretty early on, under "This is how we found out." I thought this was a good choice.

The reporters explained that they used Forest Service data based on 65 risk factors and divided the land into small sections (about 18 acres.) Then, they used census-designated places to mark communities and created a one-mile buffer zone around each one. The final hazard score was based on the average rating of burnable land within that area.

I liked that the story made the methodology accessible to the readers rather than just saying the results/findings are fact.

At the same time, the story acknowledges that wildfire hazard potential is just one part of measuring danger. The analysis dug deeper and added other factors, which is important because it really nailed that a fire hazard score alone is not enough to assess the true danger.

One problem is that the analysis focuses mainly on smaller communities and excludes any large metropolitan areas. That makes sense given the story's focus on smaller communities, but it also means the findings are not really meant to be interpreted as a whole picture of wildfire risk across the entire West.
